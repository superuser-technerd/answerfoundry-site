Published September 29, 2026

Can a service-area business with no storefront show up in AI answers?

Yes. Hiding your address doesn't disqualify you from local results or from AI answers. Google explicitly supports businesses that travel to customers, like plumbers, roofers, mobile groomers, and in-home care providers, and lets them list up to 20 service areas instead of a street address. The catch is that without a storefront pin, the signals that tell a search engine or AI assistant where you work have to come from somewhere else: your service areas, your website's city and service pages, your reviews, and mentions of you on other sites.

What this means

Google defines a service-area business as one that "visits or delivers to customers directly" but doesn't serve them at its own address. Hybrid businesses do both, like a shop that also sends crews out (Google Business Profile Help, n.d.-b). If customers don't come to you, Google tells you to remove your address and list your service areas instead. You can list cities, postal codes, or other areas, but not a radius. The whole area should stay within about two hours' drive of where you're based (Google Business Profile Help, n.d.-b).

The naive assumption is that a business without a map pin can't compete in "near me" searches or in the AI answers built on them. The better way to think about it: you've traded one strong location signal, a public address, for several weaker ones you now have to make consistent.

Who this applies to

Home services and contractors are the obvious group: HVAC, plumbing, pool service, pest control, roofing, landscaping, cleaning. It also covers mobile and in-home providers and hybrid businesses whose crews work beyond the storefront.

One edge case: Google says businesses selling age-restricted products, such as alcohol, cannabis, or weapons, need a storefront, and a service-area business can only have one profile for its whole area (Google Business Profile Help, n.d.-b). Setting up extra profiles at virtual offices to fake a local presence breaks the rules and puts the real profile at risk.

How we'd evaluate it

We run the same buyer questions a customer would ask, like "best AC repair in Winter Park" or "who does pool resurfacing near Clermont," across ChatGPT, Google AI Overviews and AI Mode, Gemini, and Perplexity. We test the towns the business says it serves, not just its home city. Then we check whether each town appears somewhere an engine can confirm it: the Business Profile service areas, a page on the website, schema markup, reviews, and outside listings.

The query itself matters. OpenAI says ChatGPT can estimate a user's general location from their IP address and rewrite a question like "good restaurants near me" into a search such as "top restaurants San Francisco" before sending it to search partners (OpenAI Help Center, n.d.). Its example is restaurants, but the pattern is the point. If "near me" becomes a city name, a business whose website never mentions that city has less to match against.

Available options

  • Set your service areas specifically. List the actual cities or postal codes you serve, up to 20, rather than one huge region. Google says to be "as specific and accurate as possible," and service-area edits can take up to 48 hours to appear (Google Business Profile Help, n.d.-b).
  • Hide the address only if customers don't come to you. Google says to hide your address only if you're a service-area business. If you hide it and don't add service areas, Google picks a local area for you (Google Business Profile Help, n.d.-a).
  • Build real service-area pages. One page per town you serve, with specific details: typical jobs, permitting notes, response times, and completed projects there. A page that only swaps the city name gives an AI engine nothing to cite.
  • Mark up your service area. Schema.org's areaServed property describes "the geographic area where a service or offered item is provided." It can be used on Organization and Service, accepts a named place, administrative area, shape, or plain text, and replaces the older serviceArea property (Schema.org, n.d.). Our post on schema markup and AI visibility covers what markup can and can't do.
  • Earn location-specific reviews and mentions. See the next section.

Benefits, limitations, and tradeoffs

Hiding your address keeps a home office private, and it's what Google's rules require when customers don't visit. The tradeoff is distance. Google says local results are "mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity," and that distance means how far a business is from the person searching (Google Business Profile Help, n.d.-c). Service areas describe where you work. They don't make you physically closer to a searcher across town.

What can partly make up for distance is prominence. Google says prominence draws on how many websites link to a business and how many reviews it has (Google Business Profile Help, n.d.-c). Local search columnist Miriam Ellis describes how businesses with stronger relevance and prominence can sometimes outrank closer competitors. Her methods include reviews that mention specific services and places, and unlinked mentions in local publications, sponsorships, and community sites (Ellis, 2025). She also notes there's no evidence that local business schema affects local rank. So treat areaServed as a way to be clear, not a ranking lever.

What we know

  • Google supports service-area and hybrid profiles, with up to 20 service areas, no radius option, and a roughly two-hour drive-time limit on the overall area (Google Business Profile Help, n.d.-b).
  • Google names relevance, distance, and prominence as the main local ranking factors, and says there's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking (Google Business Profile Help, n.d.-c).
  • ChatGPT can use approximate or shared device location for local questions. It may share location with third-party providers for things like nearby business listings, and OpenAI says placement in search results isn't guaranteed (OpenAI Help Center, n.d.).

What we haven't seen is published data comparing how often AI assistants name service-area businesses versus storefront businesses in the same category. Nothing we can cite answers that, so we won't guess at a number.

Next steps

Open your Business Profile and check three things. Is the address hidden if customers never visit? Are the service areas the towns you actually want work from? Is the category right? Then compare those towns against your website. Every town that matters should have a page with real substance, and your name, phone number, and service list should match everywhere. Our posts on Google Business Profile and AI recommendations and NAP consistency go deeper on both. Finally, ask a few AI assistants who they'd recommend for your service in each of those towns, and write down the date and the answer.

Orlando considerations

Central Florida is spread out, and a lot of service businesses work across several counties from a single home base. Customers usually search by the name of their town, like Kissimmee, Oviedo, Winter Garden, or Lake Nona, not "Orlando." A contractor whose website only says "serving Orlando and surrounding areas" gives an AI engine very little to connect to those towns. After a hurricane, people search for urgent roof and tree help in their own town, which is exactly where a clear service area matters.

Frequently asked questions

Does hiding my address hurt my chances of showing up in AI answers?

Not by itself. Google supports service-area profiles and tells businesses that don't serve customers at their address to hide it. What changes is that location signals have to come from your service areas, website, reviews, and outside mentions instead of a public address.

How many service areas can I list on Google Business Profile?

Up to 20, set by city, postal code, or other area. Google doesn't allow a radius, and it says the overall area shouldn't extend more than about two hours' drive from where the business is based.

Should I create a separate page for every city I serve?

Only for towns you actually serve and can say something specific about, like local projects, typical jobs, or permitting details. Pages that only swap the city name give AI engines nothing new to cite.

Does areaServed schema markup improve local rankings?

There's no published evidence that it does. It describes where you provide services in a machine-readable way, which helps keep your information clear and consistent, but it isn't a ranking lever on its own.
