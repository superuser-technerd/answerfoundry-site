What is AI share of voice, and how do I measure it for my business?

AI share of voice is the portion of brand mentions in AI-generated answers that belong to your business, compared with the competitors named alongside you. If AI assistants name businesses 100 times across a set of questions and 25 of those mentions are yours, your share is 25%. The idea is simple. The catch is that the tools measuring it don't all calculate it the same way.

What this means

Share of voice is an old advertising term: your slice of the total attention in a market. The AI version asks the same question about answers instead of ads. HubSpot's glossary defines it as how frequently and prominently a brand appears in responses from answer engines such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity, relative to competitors (HubSpot, n.d.).

It's tempting to treat that as one number with one meaning. It isn't. There are at least three versions in circulation:

  • Mention share. Your mentions divided by all brand mentions in the tracked answers. Similarweb defines AI share of voice this way and calls it brand mention share (Zohar Basteker, 2026).
  • Response share. The answers that mention you, divided by the answers that mention any tracked brand. Ahrefs describes this as a common basic formula (Ong, 2026).
  • Weighted share. The same counts, weighted by an estimate of how often each question gets asked. Ahrefs weights prompts this way in its own tool and shows a worked example in which three brands each have a 67% basic share but 31%, 72%, and 97% once the prompts are weighted (Ong, 2026).

Same answers, same brands, three different percentages. That's the first thing to know before anyone shows you a share-of-voice figure.

Who this applies to

Any business whose customers ask an AI assistant who to hire: law firms, contractors and home services, accounting and financial practices, real estate, clinics. It matters most in crowded categories, where an answer names several businesses and simply appearing doesn't tell you much. It matters less if you have one or two real competitors. In that case a plain yes-or-no log of who was named covers most of it.

How we'd evaluate it

We'd measure two numbers side by side, because they answer different questions. Visibility is the share of answers that mention you at all. Share of voice is your portion of every brand mention in those answers.

Here is a hypothetical example. It is not a real client or a real business. Say an Orlando roofing company runs 20 customer-style questions five times each on three assistants. That is 300 answers. The company is named in 60 of them, so its visibility is 20%. Those 300 answers contain 900 business mentions in total, and 60 are the company's. Its share of voice is about 6.7%. Both numbers are true. The second one shows how crowded the answers are.

Similarweb's published example makes the same point with real tool data: a brand that appeared in 26.72% of tracked answers held 11.87% of the brand mentions (Zohar Basteker, 2026).

Available options

  • Count it by hand. Pick 10 to 20 questions a customer would really ask, run each several times on each assistant, and tally every business named in a spreadsheet. Free, slow, and transparent.
  • Use a tracking tool. Several platforms now report AI share of voice automatically. They save the labor. Read the methodology page before you trust the percentage.
  • Have someone run it for you. The value there is a fixed, documented method, so this month's number can be compared with last month's.

Benefits, limitations, and tradeoffs

The benefit is context. A visibility figure on its own can't tell you whether you're one of two businesses named or one of nine.

The limitations are real, and we'd rather state them than sell around them. The number moves with every choice behind it: which prompts, which platforms, which competitors, which location, which month. Ahrefs puts it directly: two tools can report different figures for the same brand without either being wrong (Ong, 2026). The denominator matters too. Similarweb points out that counting only the competitors you chose to list can make your share look stronger than it is, because a business you didn't list never enters the math (Zohar Basteker, 2026).

And share of voice measures presence, not outcomes. It doesn't tell you whether a mention was favorable, whether anyone visited your site, or whether anyone called (Ong, 2026).

What we know

A single run tells you very little. In research published by SparkToro in January 2026, 600 volunteers ran 12 prompts through ChatGPT, Claude, and Google's AI a combined 2,961 times. The author reports a less than 1 in 100 chance that ChatGPT or Google's AI would give the same list of brands in any two responses, and found the order of those lists even less stable (Fishkin, 2026).

Repeated runs do tell you something. The same study found that how often a brand appears across many runs of a prompt was fairly consistent, and concluded that measuring that percentage is "(probably) a reasonable way" to gauge how prominent a business is in an AI's consideration set. The author suggests asking at least 60 to 100 times to see an AI's real set of recommendations (Fishkin, 2026). One note on that source: the research was run with a partner who works at an AI-tracking company, which the author discloses.

Interest in the metric is rising. Semrush's U.S. keyword database showed roughly 210 monthly searches for "ai share of voice" when we checked on November 24, 2026, with its 12-month trend line at its highest point in the most recent months.

What we don't know: no AI company publishes how often it names a given business, so every share-of-voice figure is an outside estimate built from sampled prompts. That includes ours.

Next steps

  • Write down 10 to 20 questions your customers would actually ask an assistant.
  • Run each one several times on each assistant you care about, and log every business named.
  • Calculate visibility and share of voice separately.
  • Record the scope next to the number: prompts, platforms, location, dates, and which businesses you counted.
  • Repeat monthly with the same prompts and the same method. The trend is more useful than any single reading.

Orlando considerations

Location changes the answer. The businesses an assistant names for a roofer or a family law attorney in Winter Park aren't the ones it names in Kissimmee or Lake Nona, so a share-of-voice figure measured without a place in the prompt describes a market you may not serve. In Central Florida, where many service businesses cover several cities, we'd measure each service area as its own set of prompts and keep the results separate. Blending them hides the area where you're missing.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good AI share of voice?

There is no universal benchmark. The number depends on how many competitors are counted, which prompts are tracked, and which formula is used, so a figure from one tool or one market can't be compared with a figure from another. The useful comparison is your own number against the same competitors, measured the same way, over time.

Is AI share of voice the same as AI visibility?

No. Visibility is the percentage of AI answers that mention your business at all. Share of voice is your portion of all the brand mentions in those answers. A business can appear in a quarter of the answers and still hold a small share of voice if each answer also names five or six competitors.

Why do two tools show different AI share of voice numbers for my business?

Usually because they are measuring different things. Tools differ on the prompts they run, the AI platforms they check, the location, whether they count only the competitors you listed or every brand named, and whether prompts are weighted by estimated demand. Different inputs produce different percentages, and neither has to be wrong.

Can AnswerFoundry guarantee a higher AI share of voice?

No. ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Claude, and Google's AI features are third-party systems that choose which businesses to name, and no one outside those companies controls that. What we can do is measure where you stand with a documented method, work on the signals within your control, and re-measure the same way so any change is real.
