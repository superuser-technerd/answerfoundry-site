How does AI visual search work, and what does it mean for my business?

AI visual search starts with a picture instead of words. You take or upload a photo, the system identifies what’s in it, runs several web searches about those things, and writes one answer with links. For your business, the key point is that the answer still comes from the web, so your pages and images are the raw material. Google says its AI search features need no special optimization beyond normal SEO fundamentals, and it hasn’t published a separate playbook for visual search. Nothing here guarantees you’ll appear.

What this means

In Google’s version, two systems work together. Google Lens identifies the objects in a photo, and a Gemini model reasons about them alongside whatever question you typed. AI Mode then uses query fan-out, sending out multiple searches about the whole image and each object in it, and combines the results into one response with links (Stein, 2025). A Google explainer describes the model as the part that “sees” the image and the search backend as a library of web results (McHugh-Johnson, 2026).

That second half is what matters to a business owner. A photo doesn’t get answered from the photo alone. It gets turned into searches, and those searches land on ordinary web pages. If someone photographs a dish, a product, or a broken part and asks what it is and who can help, your site can only be part of the answer if it describes that thing clearly enough to be found.

Who this applies to

Mostly businesses whose offering can be photographed: retailers, restaurants, salons, furniture and home-goods shops, landscapers, contractors, and repair companies. A homeowner can photograph a failing water heater valve as easily as a shopper can photograph a pair of shoes. It matters less if what you sell is purely advisory and nobody would ever point a camera at it.

How we’d evaluate it

We’d start by testing it the way a customer would. Take the pictures your customers are likely to take, such as your best-known product, a signature dish, or a common problem you fix, and run them through Google Lens and AI Mode. Note what gets identified, which businesses or sites get linked, and whether a follow-up question like “who sells this in Orlando?” changes the result. Then we’d look at your site: are the relevant photos real images in the page’s HTML, is there text nearby naming the item, and does a page exist for that product or service at all?

Available options

  • Publish your own clear photos. Show the products, food, and work people actually ask about, rather than generic stock images.
  • Put images near text that names them. A photo of a specific item belongs on a page that names and explains that item.
  • Use standard image markup. Use HTML image elements, not CSS backgrounds, with descriptive alt text.
  • Keep key facts in text. Prices, hours, and service areas shouldn’t live only inside an image.
  • If you sell products, keep listings accurate. Current names, prices, and availability give shopping results something reliable to draw on.
  • Keep your profile photos current. Our post on Google Business Profile photos covers what’s known and what isn’t.

Benefits, limitations, and tradeoffs

The upside is that most of this is work you’d want anyway. Better photos and clearer product and service pages help customers and regular search, whether or not visual search ever sends you anyone. The limits are real, though. Google doesn’t explain how it picks which sites to link in a visual answer, and these features change quickly. An engine may also identify a product correctly and still send the shopper to a national retailer. Original photography costs money too, so start with the handful of items customers ask about most.

What we know

Google has described the mechanics in some detail. When AI Mode gained image understanding in 2025, Google said Lens identifies each object and fan-out then issues multiple queries about the image and its objects (Stein, 2025). Later that year it described a “visual search fan-out” technique that also recognizes subtle details and secondary objects, not just the main subject, and said each image result carries a link. For shopping, it said results draw on a Shopping Graph of more than 50 billion product listings (Stein & Rincon, 2025). Google’s own product page says Lens can look up who makes an item, what it costs, and where to buy it, and can be used for artwork, murals, and landmarks (Google, n.d.).

Usage is substantial by Google’s own account. A June 2026 Think with Google article says Lens powers more than 25 billion visual searches a month, citing internal Google data from April 2025, and that more than 1 in 6 AI Mode queries are now entirely non-text (Khattar & Nupur, 2026). Those are Google’s figures, from an article written for marketers, and no outside party has verified them.

On the website side, Google says there are no additional requirements or special optimizations for AI Overviews or AI Mode, and that pages must be indexed and eligible for a snippet (Google Search Central, n.d.-a). Its image guidance says standard HTML image elements help crawlers, that it doesn’t index CSS images, that images should sit near relevant text, and that it uses alt text along with computer vision and page content to understand an image (Google Search Central, n.d.-b). That guidance doesn’t mention Lens or AI Mode, so applying it to visual search is a reasonable inference, not a statement from Google.

Next steps

Pick three things customers are likely to photograph and search them in Google Lens today. If your business doesn’t appear, check whether your site has a page that clearly names each one, with a real photo of it in the HTML. Fix those pages first, then repeat the same test in a month and compare. Our guide to Google AI Overviews covers the broader basics.

Orlando considerations

Orlando’s visitors are often in unfamiliar places looking at unfamiliar things, which is exactly when pointing a phone at something is easier than typing. Restaurants, attractions, and shops near International Drive, Disney Springs, or downtown should make sure their names, menus, and signature items are written out on their sites. Businesses that serve Kissimmee, Winter Park, or Sanford should name those areas in text, because a photo alone tells a search system little about who serves a given neighborhood.

Frequently asked questions

Can I submit my photos to Google Lens or AI Mode directly?

Google doesn’t describe a submission process for Lens or AI Mode. Its documentation says pages need to be indexed and eligible to show in Search with a snippet to appear in AI features, and its image guidance covers how to help Google find and understand images on your site.

Does alt text help with AI visual search?

It may help indirectly, but Google hasn’t said so specifically. Google’s image SEO guidance says it uses alt text along with computer vision and page content to understand an image. That guidance doesn’t mention Lens or AI Mode, so treat the connection as a reasonable inference.

Is visual search only for retailers?

No, though shopping is the clearest use today. Google says Lens can help people find who makes an item, what it costs, and where to buy it, and can also be used for artwork, murals, and landmarks. Any business whose products, food, or work people might photograph has a reason to pay attention.

Can AnswerFoundry guarantee my business appears when someone searches with a photo?

No. AI engines are third-party systems that nobody outside those companies controls. What we can do is test the images your customers are likely to search with, check whether your site gives AI systems clear material to work from, and track what changes over time.
