Published October 20, 2026

How do law firms show up in AI search results?

Mostly through sources other than their own websites. When someone asks ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Google’s AI Mode to recommend a lawyer, the answer leans heavily on legal directories such as Avvo, Justia, Martindale, Super Lawyers, and Best Lawyers, along with Google Business Profiles and the firm’s own attorney and practice-area pages. Firms that are consistent and well documented across those sources are easier for an AI engine to name with confidence. No firm can guarantee a spot, though.

What this means

A traditional Google search gives a potential client a list of links and lets them choose. An AI answer makes a shortlist for them. To decide whom to name, the engine pulls from sources it can find and cross-check: directory profiles, ratings, map listings, bar-related information, and the firm’s website. When those sources agree on who you are, where you practice, and what kinds of cases you take, you’re a safer recommendation. When they disagree, or when your firm barely appears outside its own site, you’re easy to leave out.

This is why a firm can have a good website and still be missing from AI answers. In legal searches, the AI engine often trusts what other sites say about you as much as what you say about yourself.

Who this applies to

This matters most to firms that get clients from people searching on their own: personal injury, family law, criminal defense, estate planning, immigration, and bankruptcy. These are the practice areas where someone with an urgent problem types or speaks a question like “who’s a good divorce lawyer near me?” Firms that work almost entirely by referral, or that serve a small group of institutional clients, will see less direct impact. Even so, a referred client may check an AI engine before making the call.

How we’d evaluate it

We start by running the questions your clients actually ask, across several AI engines and with several phrasings, and recording who gets named and which sources are cited. Then we compare the firm’s footprint against those sources. Is the firm listed on the directories the engines cite, with matching names, addresses, and practice areas? Does each attorney who meets with clients have an accurate Google Business Profile where that’s allowed? Do the attorney bios show real credentials and experience? Does each practice area have its own clear page? The goal is to find specific gaps, not to produce a generic score.

Available options

  • Clean up directory profiles. Claim and complete your profiles on the major legal directories, and make the firm name, address, phone number, and practice areas match everywhere.
  • Get Google Business Profile right. Google allows public-facing practitioners, including lawyers, to have their own profiles. It has specific naming rules, and it doesn’t allow separate profiles for each specialization (Google Business Profile Help, n.d.).
  • Strengthen attorney bios and practice-area pages. Use clear, factual pages that name the person, their credentials, where they practice, and what kinds of matters they handle.
  • Earn outside mentions. Bar association activity, local press, and published articles give AI engines independent sources to corroborate your firm.

Benefits, limitations, and tradeoffs

Directory and profile work is fairly low-cost and also helps traditional local search. The limitation is that much of the source landscape belongs to someone else. Directories set their own rules, some sell paid placements, and AI engines change which sources they favor without notice. Legal content also has to follow bar advertising rules, so the sharp, claim-heavy copy some marketers recommend for AI visibility may be off-limits for lawyers. And showing up in an AI answer is only the first step: the firm still has to answer the phone well.

What we know

Google says there are no extra requirements or special optimizations to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode. A page needs to be indexed and eligible to show in Search with a snippet. Google also says both features may use “query fan-out,” running several related searches to build one answer (Google Search Central, 2025). For legal topics, Google’s guidance on helpful content says its systems give “even more weight” to content that shows strong experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness on “Your Money or Your Life” topics. That category covers subjects that can significantly affect someone’s finances or safety, and legal problems often do. Google also recommends clear bylines and author background information (Google Search Central, 2026).

On the AI side, a 2026 report by Haute Lawyer Network and 5W audited legal queries across ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, and Google AI Mode in eight practice areas. It found that seven directories, Chambers, Legal 500, Super Lawyers, Best Lawyers, Martindale, Avvo, and Justia, dominated the citations, with individual firms appearing mostly inside those directories. The same report puts the share of legal queries that trigger Google AI Overviews at 23.6%, and 57.9% for question-style queries (Haute Lawyer Network & 5W, 2026). 5W sells generative engine optimization services, so treat its findings as one useful data point rather than settled fact. Finally, a LEX Reception and OnePoll survey of 6,000 respondents found that 89% would rather speak to a real person than to AI when contacting a law firm (LEX Reception, 2026). LEX Reception sells reception services. The takeaway still holds: being discovered through AI doesn’t replace a human intake.

Next steps

Ask three or four AI engines the questions a potential client in your practice area would ask, with your city included. Write down which firms and which sources come back. Then check your own listings on those same sources for accuracy and completeness. Fix anything that doesn’t match before you produce any new content.

Orlando considerations

Florida lawyers have an extra layer to think about. The Florida Bar regulates lawyer advertising, including firm websites and social media pages, under Rules 4-7.11 through 4-7.22, and publishes a checklist specifically for websites and social media (The Florida Bar, n.d.). Any content written to help AI engines understand your firm should be reviewed against those rules. Central Florida also has a dense market in personal injury and family law, so the gap between being named and being left out of an AI shortlist can come down to the accuracy and completeness of your listings.

Frequently asked questions

Does my law firm need to be on Avvo, Justia, or Super Lawyers to show up in AI answers?

Not necessarily, but legal directories are a large part of what AI engines cite for lawyer-finding questions, according to a 2026 audit by Haute Lawyer Network and 5W. A complete, accurate directory profile gives an AI engine another independent source that confirms who you are and what you practice.

Should each attorney at my firm have their own Google Business Profile?

Google’s guidelines allow public-facing individual practitioners, including lawyers, to have their own profiles if they’re directly contactable at a verified location during business hours. Follow Google’s naming rules, and don’t create separate profiles for each practice area.

Do Florida Bar advertising rules apply to content I write for AI search?

The Florida Bar regulates lawyer advertising, including law firm websites and social media pages, under Rules 4-7.11 through 4-7.22. Content written to be cited by AI engines is still your firm’s communication, so the same rules apply. Check with the Bar’s resources or your ethics counsel.

Can AnswerFoundry guarantee my firm will be recommended by ChatGPT or Google AI?

No. AI engines are third-party systems that nobody outside those companies controls. What we can do is find the gaps in the signals you do control, such as directory profiles, Google Business Profile, attorney bios, and practice-area pages, and track whether AI answers change over time.
