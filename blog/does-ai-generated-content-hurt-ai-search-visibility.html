Does AI-generated content hurt your AI search visibility?

Not inherently — but "not inherently" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Google's own guidance says content isn't ranked or penalized based on whether AI wrote it. A 16-month experiment that published 2,000 AI-generated articles across brand-new domains found something more specific: 80% of the sites ranked for 100+ keywords within the first month, then 97% of those pages had fallen out of the top 100 within three months. The difference wasn't AI versus human. It was authority, editing, and originality — and pure AI output usually has none of the three.

What this means

Google has said plainly, and reaffirmed since, that content is not ranked or penalized based on whether it was created by AI or a human — what's evaluated is the quality, usefulness, and intent of the content (Google Search Central, 2023). That guidance hasn't changed. What has changed is how much AI content is actually in the mix: Originality.ai's ongoing tracking study found AI-generated text present in 17.31% of the top 20 Google results as of September 2025, down from an all-time high of 19.56% two months earlier, but still roughly three and a half times higher than the under-5% level recorded in early 2023 (Originality.ai, 2025).

So AI content isn't rare, and it isn't automatically penalized. The real question isn't "did AI write this," it's "does this page hold up once the novelty of ranking wears off" — and that's a much narrower test than most people assume.

Who this applies to

This matters most for businesses publishing blog posts, service pages, or FAQ content at volume using AI tools with little or no human review — a common shortcut for owners trying to keep up with a content calendar. It also matters for anyone tempted to match a competitor who appears to be publishing AI content successfully; the appearance of early success is exactly what the research below says not to trust. It matters least for businesses using AI as a drafting aid inside a normal editorial process — outline, fact-check, rewrite, publish — since that's the pattern the evidence says holds up.

How we'd evaluate it

A review of AI-assisted content checks whether a real person visibly edited it — specific numbers, names, local details, and direct claims that a generic AI pass wouldn't produce on its own. It checks whether the page adds anything an existing top-ranking page doesn't already say, since near-duplicate content is one of the most common reasons AI pages lose their early rankings. It also checks publishing velocity relative to site age and authority: a handful of well-edited AI-assisted posts on an established site reads very differently to search and AI systems than fifty unedited posts published in a month on a brand-new domain.

Available options

  • Use AI to draft, have a person edit before publishing. The evidence consistently favors this pattern over either pure AI publishing or a total ban on AI assistance.
  • Audit what's already live. A content review can flag pages that read as unedited AI output before they become a liability, rather than after a ranking or citation drop.
  • Slow down publishing velocity on new or lower-authority pages. Volume without editorial review is the single most consistent predictor of the ranking collapse described below.

Benefits, limitations, and tradeoffs

The benefit of understanding this distinction is that it removes a false choice — you don't have to choose between "never use AI" and "publish AI content at scale and hope." The limitation is that almost all of the hard evidence here is about Google's traditional organic search ranking, which is well-documented. How individual AI answer engines like ChatGPT or Perplexity specifically weigh whether a source page was AI-written when deciding what to cite is not publicly documented the same way, so treat that connection as directional, not confirmed — we're honest about that gap rather than implying more certainty than exists.

What we know

Google's official guidance, first published in February 2023 and still current, states that using automation — including AI — to generate content primarily to manipulate search rankings violates its spam policies, while appropriate use of AI or automation, similar to long-standing helpful automation like weather forecasts or sports scores, does not (Google Search Central, 2023).

Search Engine Land's 16-month experiment, conducted with SE Ranking's research team, published 2,000 AI-generated articles across new, zero-authority domains. About 71% of pages were indexed within 36 days, and 80% of the test sites ranked for at least 100 keywords within the first month. By roughly three months after publication, only 3% of pages remained in the top 100, down from 28% at the one-month mark. The researchers attributed the collapse to a lack of domain authority and backlinks, a lack of first-hand expertise and experience, and high similarity to content that already existed (Babiak, 2026).

Originality.ai's ongoing study, which has sampled the top 20 results for 500 informational keywords every two months since 2019, found AI-generated content present in 17.31% of top results as of September 2025 — evidence that a meaningful share of AI-assisted content does persist in rankings, consistent with Google's position that origin isn't the deciding factor (Originality.ai, 2025).

Next steps

If you're using AI to help produce content, build in a real editorial pass before anything publishes: add a specific detail, number, or example the AI didn't generate, and confirm the page says something a top-ranking competitor doesn't already say. If you're unsure whether existing content on your site would read as unedited AI output to a person or a search system, that's a fast, cheap thing to check before it becomes a ranking or citation problem.

Orlando considerations

Central Florida's service categories — med spas, dental practices, law firms, home services — are dense and competitive enough that duplicate or thin content stands out faster than it would in a less crowded market. If several similarly positioned local businesses are all publishing AI-drafted FAQ or service pages with minimal editing, the research suggests those pages are exactly the ones most likely to lose visibility first, which is a real opportunity for a business willing to add the editorial step others are skipping.

Frequently asked questions

Does Google always penalize AI content?

No. Google's official guidance states content isn't ranked or penalized based on whether it was produced by AI or a human — what's evaluated is quality, usefulness, and intent. The penalty applies specifically to automation used primarily to manipulate rankings, which Google classifies as scaled content abuse.

Can I use AI to help write content and still rank well?

The evidence says yes, if a real person edits it, fact-checks it, and adds something the AI couldn't generate on its own — original data, direct experience, or a specific point of view. What consistently fails is publishing AI output at volume with no review.

Is this the same as how ChatGPT or Perplexity treat AI-written pages?

Not necessarily, and that's a real limitation of this evidence. The research cited here concerns Google's traditional organic ranking system. Individual AI answer engines don't publish equivalent data on whether they favor or avoid citing AI-written source content, so treat this as directionally relevant, not as confirmed AI-engine behavior.

How can I tell if content on my own site reads as "obviously AI"?

Look for generic claims with no specific numbers, names, or examples; a lack of any first-hand detail about your business or customers; and repetitive sentence structure. If a competitor could publish the exact same paragraph with their name swapped in, it's a signal the content needs a real edit.
