Published August 24, 2026

How long should a page be to get cited by AI search?

Short answer: there's no single number, and the platform you're optimizing for changes the answer completely. Ahrefs studied 174,048 pages behind 560,346 AI Overviews and found a Spearman correlation of just 0.04 between word count and citation — essentially zero (Ahrefs, 2025). ChatGPT tells a different story: pages over 2,900 words get cited 59% more often than pages under 800 words (Passionfruit, 2026). And Google's own documentation addresses the instinct to hit a target word count head-on: "No, we don't" have one (Google Search Central, 2025).

What this means

AI engines don't retrieve content the same way, so a single "ideal length" doesn't exist. Google AI Overviews build answers through a query fan-out process: a search gets split into several sub-queries, and citations get pulled from whichever pages answer each piece best. A 500-word page that nails one specific sub-query has the same shot as a 5,000-word guide, which is why word count barely moves the needle there — the correlation Ahrefs measured, 0.04, is close to noise (Ahrefs, 2025). ChatGPT works differently. It runs live web searches and tends to favor pages with more extractable material, so longer pages that pack in data points, tables, and detail get cited at a meaningfully higher rate (Passionfruit, 2026). Google's own Search Central guidance speaks to the underlying question directly, listing "writing to a particular word count because you've heard Google has a preferred word count" as a habit to avoid — flatly stating, "No, we don't" (Google Search Central, 2025).

Who this applies to

This matters for any business deciding how much to write for a page meant to get cited — a service page, an FAQ, a blog post like this one. It matters most for businesses actively producing content for AI visibility rather than treating every page the same way, since the right length genuinely depends on which engine sends the most relevant traffic.

How we'd evaluate it

A proper read checks length against the platform, not against a blanket target. That means testing whether the customer questions your content should answer are the kind Google AI Overviews handle with sub-queries, which favors focused, specific pages, or the kind ChatGPT tends to research more broadly, which favors depth. It also means looking at structure within the page: Ahrefs found that citation position doesn't shift meaningfully with length either, a 0.04 correlation with position, the same as with citation rate overall, so a long page doesn't win a better spot just by being long (Ahrefs, 2025).

Available options

  • Write to the topic, not a target. Cover what the question actually requires and stop. Google's documentation names hitting an arbitrary word count as a red flag, not a strength (Google Search Central, 2025).
  • Structure in short, self-contained sections. Independent research on ChatGPT citation patterns found that pages built from roughly 120-180 word sections earn substantially more citations than pages with sections under 50 words, regardless of total length (Passionfruit, 2026).
  • Match format to intent. Ahrefs' data shows core pages and product listings are cited with median lengths around 300-550 words, while articles median around 1,166 words — the format should follow the content type, not the other way around (Ahrefs, 2025).

Benefits, limitations, and tradeoffs

The benefit of this approach is that it removes a fake constraint — nobody has to pad a page to 2,000 words "for the algorithm," and nobody has to chop a genuinely complex topic down to hit some arbitrary short-form target. The limitation is that it requires knowing which AI platform actually matters to your customers, since the same page can be well-suited for one engine and poorly suited for another. The tradeoff shows up in resourcing: writing two versions of the same content, a focused one and a comprehensive one, isn't realistic for most small businesses, so the practical move is picking a length that matches the topic and letting structure do the rest of the work.

What we know

Three points hold up across the available research. Google's own team is explicit that content length is not something its systems reward directly: "Are you writing to a particular word count because you've heard or read that Google has a preferred word count? (No, we don't.)" appears verbatim in its people-first content guidance, last updated December 2025 (Google Search Central, 2025). Ahrefs' study of 174,048 pages cited across 560,346 AI Overviews found the correlation between word count and citation is 0.04, and more than half, 53.4%, of cited pages are under 1,000 words (Ahrefs, 2025). Separate analysis of ChatGPT citation patterns found the opposite pull on that one platform: articles over 2,900 words are cited 59% more often than pages under 800 words, an effect the researchers attribute mainly to the data tables, comparisons, and structured sections that longer pages tend to contain rather than length by itself (Passionfruit, 2026).

Next steps

Stop writing to a word count target. Write enough to actually answer the question your customer is asking, break it into sections short enough to stand alone, and let the topic's natural scope decide the final length. If ChatGPT visibility matters more to your business than Google's AI features, lean toward more comprehensive pages with real data in them — but don't pad content that doesn't need it just to hit a number.

Orlando considerations

Local service categories — dental, legal, home services, med spas — tend to have pages that answer narrow, specific questions, like "do you treat X" or "how much does Y cost." That's exactly the kind of page Google's fan-out process can cite regardless of length, so Orlando businesses shouldn't assume they need long-form content to compete for AI visibility in local categories. Where longer content earns its keep locally is on genuinely comprehensive resources, like full service explainers or FAQ hubs, where more real content actually serves the reader.

Frequently asked questions

Does a longer blog post always rank better in AI search?

No. Ahrefs' study of 174,048 cited pages found essentially no relationship between word count and Google AI Overview citations, a 0.04 correlation, and 53.4% of cited pages were under 1,000 words. Longer content only helps meaningfully on ChatGPT, and even there the lift comes mainly from the data and structure long pages tend to contain, not length by itself.

Does Google penalize short content?

No, and Google says so directly. Its Search Central documentation lists writing to a specific word count as a sign of search-engine-first thinking to avoid, not something its ranking systems reward.

Should I write differently for ChatGPT than for Google AI Overviews?

If ChatGPT visibility matters most to your business, yes, comprehensive pages with real data and roughly 120-180 word sections perform better there. For Google's AI features, focus on directly answering specific questions rather than expanding length.

How short can a page be and still get cited?

Short is not automatically ruled out. Ahrefs found pages under 350 words still land in the top three citation positions in over 95% of the AI Overview citations they receive, competing on equal footing with much longer content.
