Depth of field and hyperfocal distance — getting the whole landscape sharp

A landscape with a boulder at your feet and a peak on the horizon has to be sharp from front to back, and the only way to know whether it will be is to calculate it. The Depth of field tool does the calculation. This guide explains what the numbers mean and how to use them in the field.

What depth of field is

Only one plane is truly in focus. Everything in front of and behind it is slightly blurred, and "sharp" means the blur is smaller than the eye can see in the final print. That threshold is the circle of confusion, and it depends on the sensor size: Lightpath uses 0.030 mm for full frame, 0.020 mm for APS-C and 0.015 mm for Micro Four Thirds — the conventional values for a print viewed at normal distance.

Depth of field gets deeper when you:

  • Stop down — a smaller aperture (bigger f-number).
  • Go wider — a shorter focal length.
  • Focus further away.

It gets shallower when you do the opposite, which is what portrait photographers want and landscape photographers usually do not.

Hyperfocal distance

For a given lens and aperture there is one focus distance at which everything from half that distance to infinity is acceptably sharp. That is the hyperfocal distance, and it is the single most useful number in landscape photography:

H = f² ÷ (N × c) + f

with f the focal length, N the f-number and c the circle of confusion. For a 24 mm lens at f/8 on full frame, H ≈ 2.4 m. Focus at 2.4 m and everything from 1.2 m to the horizon is sharp. Focus on the mountain at infinity instead and you throw away the foreground for nothing.

How to use the tool

  1. Open Tools › Depth of field. Choose your sensor format.
  2. Enter focal length, aperture and the distance you plan to focus at.
  3. Read the near and far limits and the hyperfocal distance. The tool respects your metric/imperial setting.
  4. If the near limit is further than your foreground, either stop down, focus closer, or accept that the foreground will be soft.

The diffraction trade-off

Stopping down past about f/11 on full frame (f/8 on APS-C, f/5.6 on Micro Four Thirds) starts to soften the whole image through diffraction. Depth of field keeps growing but peak sharpness falls. The sweet spot for most landscapes is f/8 to f/11 with the lens focused at the hyperfocal distance. If that still cannot hold a very close foreground, focus-stack two frames rather than shooting f/22.

Field tips

  • Distances are measured from the sensor plane, not the front of the lens. For close foregrounds this matters.
  • Focus by distance scale or by magnified live view on an object at the hyperfocal distance; autofocus on the horizon puts the focus plane in the wrong place.
  • For night sky work the calculation is irrelevant — you focus on a star at infinity and open the aperture as wide as it goes. Use the Stars tool instead (guide 5).
