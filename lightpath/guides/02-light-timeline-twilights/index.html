Golden hour, blue hour and the three twilights — using the Light timeline

Photographers talk about "golden hour" as if it were a fixed 60 minutes. It is not. Its length depends on your latitude and the season: near the equator it can be twenty minutes; in Iceland in June it can last most of the night. Lightpath's Light timeline exists so you never have to guess.

What the strip shows

The timeline is one 24-hour strip for the selected day and location. The background bands are the sky states, computed from the sun's altitude:

BandSun altitudeWhat it looks like
Daylightabove +6°Full sun, hard shadows
Golden hour+6° down to sunset (−0.8°)Warm, long shadows, low contrast
Blue hour (civil twilight)sunset to −6°Deep even blue, city lights glowing
Nautical twilight−6° to −12°Horizon still visible, first bright stars
Astronomical twilight−12° to −18°Sky nearly dark, faint stars appearing
Full darkbelow −18°Night

Sunset is taken as −0.8°, not 0°, because atmospheric refraction lifts the sun's disc by about that much when it sits on the horizon. These are the exact thresholds the app uses.

The solid line is the sun's altitude through the day; the dotted line is the moon's. The lighter shaded region low on the strip marks when the Milky Way core is above the horizon. The blue tick near the bottom is the core window — the hours when the core is up and the sky is dark.

Why the thresholds matter

Civil, nautical and astronomical twilight are not photographer's slang; they are formal definitions used by the U.S. Naval Observatory and by every almanac. Civil twilight ends when the sun's centre is 6° below the horizon, nautical at 12°, astronomical at 18°. For you, they map neatly onto three different kinds of photograph: the blue-hour cityscape (civil), the twilight seascape with a visible horizon line (nautical), and the point after which the Milky Way is worth shooting (astronomical ends).

How to use it

  1. Open Today and find the Light timeline card. The vertical line is now.
  2. Drag the scrubber. The altitude readouts for sun, moon and core update, and so does the Shot Score above.
  3. Scroll down to Coming up. Every band boundary is listed as an event with a time — Golden hour begins, Sunset, Blue hour to 19:37, Full dark — so you can set an alarm without reading the graph.
  4. Tap the date to plan a different day. The strip redraws for that date at the same location.

Field tips

  • Arrive at least 30 minutes before Golden hour begins. The best colour often comes in the last ten minutes before sunset and the first ten after, and you want to be composed and metered by then.
  • Blue hour after sunset is short at mid-latitudes — often 20 to 25 minutes. The timeline tells you exactly how long you have.
  • In midsummer at high latitudes the strip may show no Night band at all. That is not a bug; the sun never reaches −18°.
