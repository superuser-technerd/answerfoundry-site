Lightpath guides

Lining up the sun or moon with a landmark — the Planner and Return Alerts

The most memorable landscape photographs are often alignments: the sun setting exactly in the notch of a canyon, the full moon rising behind a lighthouse, the last light threading a city street. These are not luck. They are geometry, and the Planner is built to do the geometry for you.

Azimuth is the whole game

Every celestial body has two coordinates at any moment: altitude (how high above the horizon) and azimuth (compass direction, 0° north through 90° east, 180° south, 270° west). Lightpath draws the azimuth of the sun, the moon and the Milky Way core as lines from your spot on the map. Where a line crosses your landmark, that is where the body will appear from where you stand.

Because the sun's setting azimuth swings roughly 60° between the June and December solstices at mid-latitudes, most alignments only happen a few days a year. The trick is finding those days.

Planning an alignment

  1. Open Planner. Set the location to where you will stand — not where the landmark is. Use the picker or a saved spot.
  2. Drag the time slider until the sun (solid line) or moon (dotted line) reaches the horizon; the altitude readout under the map goes to about 0°.
  3. Look at where the line crosses the landmark. If it misses, change the date and try again. The line pivots day by day.
  4. When it lines up, note the time and azimuth shown in the cards below the map.
  5. Tap AR sky view to sanity-check against the real scene, then Save as recipe.

Return Alerts — let the app find the next one

If you save a recipe while the sun is near the horizon, Lightpath stores the alignment: which body, rise or set, and the azimuth to within about a degree. From then on the Return alert card on the Today tab computes the next date the same body will rise or set at that same azimuth from that spot, and schedules a local notification the evening before. This is how you turn one lucky sunset into an annual appointment.

The search runs up to 420 days ahead. Sun alignments recur roughly twice a year (once on the way to each solstice); moon alignments are messier because the moon's cycle is 18.6 years, so the app searches night by night.

Field tips

  • Set the location precisely. Moving your standing point 50 metres can shift the alignment by several degrees at close range.
  • Distant landmarks are forgiving; close ones are not. A ridge 10 km away tolerates a degree of azimuth error; a building 200 m away does not.
  • A 1° error at the horizon is about two sun diameters. The app rounds saved azimuths to the nearest degree, so treat the return date as "within a day or two", and check the Planner as it approaches.
  • Remember refraction: the sun appears about half a degree higher than its geometric position when it is on the horizon, so it "sets" slightly later than pure geometry predicts.
