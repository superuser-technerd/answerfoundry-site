Planning a time-lapse — interval, frames and clip length

A time-lapse is a video assembled from still photographs taken at a fixed interval. Sunsets, cloud movement over a ridge and the Milky Way wheeling overhead all suit it, and all of them fail for the same reason: the shoot ends before the sequence is long enough, or the interval was wrong for the subject. The Time-lapse tool turns "I want a 20-second clip of this sunset" into "shoot every 5 seconds for 40 minutes".

The three numbers

  • Interval — seconds between frames.
  • Duration — how long you shoot, in minutes.
  • Frame rate — the playback rate of the finished clip, usually 24, 25 or 30 fps.

They are connected by simple arithmetic:

frames = duration × 60 ÷ interval
clip length (s) = frames ÷ fps

Enter the interval, duration and frame rate in Tools › Time-lapse and it returns the frame count and clip length. A 60-minute shoot at a 5-second interval gives 720 frames, which is 30 seconds at 24 fps. If you wanted a 10-second clip you could stop after 20 minutes — or shoot the full hour at a 15-second interval.

Choosing the interval for the subject

SubjectInterval
Fast clouds, busy street1–3 s
Sunset, slow clouds, tide3–8 s
Shadows crossing a landscape10–30 s
Milky Way, star trails20–40 s (shutter plus a few seconds)

Too short an interval wastes card space and battery; too long and motion becomes jerky. For the night sky the interval is dictated by the shutter: use the Stars tool (guide 5) to get the NPF shutter, then add 2–3 seconds for the camera to write the file.

Smooth motion: the shutter angle idea

Cinema convention is a shutter open for half the frame interval — the "180-degree shutter" — which gives natural motion blur. Applied to time-lapse, the shutter should stay open for a good fraction of the interval: at a 5-second interval, a 2-second exposure blurs moving cloud into smooth streaks, while a 1/500 exposure produces a jittery, strobing sky. Use an ND filter (guide 8) to force the long exposure in daylight.

Planning the window with Lightpath

  1. On Today, use the Light timeline and Coming up to bracket the sequence — from Golden hour begins to Blue hour to 19:37, say, is about 70 minutes.
  2. Put 70 minutes into the Time-lapse tool with your interval and frame rate. Check the clip length is what you want.
  3. Note the frame count. At roughly 30–50 MB per raw file, 840 frames is 25–40 GB; make sure the card and battery can take it.
  4. Start a few minutes early. You can trim the beginning; you cannot recover the moment the colour peaked if you were still setting up.

Field tips

  • Lock everything: manual exposure, manual focus, manual white balance, and auto-ISO off. Any automatic setting produces flicker between frames.
  • For day-to-night sequences ("holy grail" time-lapses) you have to ramp exposure by hand or with an intervalometer that supports it. Ramp in small steps and smooth in post.
  • Turn off image stabilisation on a tripod and disable long-exposure noise reduction; both introduce frame-to-frame variation.
