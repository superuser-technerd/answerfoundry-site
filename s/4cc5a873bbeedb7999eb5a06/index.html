01 Executive summary

Meridian is a well-run practice with a data problem, not a quality problem. It holds a 5.0 rating across 214 Google reviews, the owner responds to reviews, the state license is clear with no discipline or complaints on file, and it ranks first organically for its signature device treatment. It is also close to invisible in the places AI answers are actually assembled from.

The split is unusually clean, and it is the most useful thing in this report. Where Google's own first-party data decides the answer — Maps, the local pack — Meridian performs well, appearing in both local queries tested and ranking second in its own neighborhood. Where the answer is assembled from third-party editorial and directory text — category listicles, review aggregators, vertical directories — Meridian appears in almost nothing. It was absent from all four "best med spa in Orlando" roundups examined, has no RealSelf presence, and its entries on three aggregators are unclaimed and carry review counts between 2 and 38 against a real count of 214.

That matters because AI assistants lean heavily on the second category. A business can own the map and still never be named in a generated answer.

Underneath that sit three compounding technical issues. The service pages — the pages that would answer "where do I get X in Orlando" — carry no structured data at all. The homepage has no H1 and its only Person markup names the web developer's admin account rather than the clinician. And the 214 reviews, the practice's single strongest asset, live inside a third-party iframe, meaning they are invisible to any crawler or model reading the page.

None of this is exotic. All of it is fixable, and the highest-value fixes cost nothing but attention.

04 Prompt methodology

Prompts are written the way a prospective patient would ask, not the way a marketer would search. Three families are used, and the mix is fixed before testing begins so results cannot be curated after the fact.

FamilyWhat it measuresCount
Category discovery — "best med spa Orlando"Whether the business is a candidate answer at all for high-intent, unbranded demand6
Service-specific — "[treatment] Orlando FL"Whether individual service pages surface independently of the brand2
Adjacent / qualifier — ownership model, ZIP, neighborhoodWhether the business is retrievable when a customer adds a constraint1
Branded control — "[business name] reviews"Baseline: confirms the business is retrievable at all, isolating a discovery failure from an indexing failure1

Rules of the method

  • The prompt set is fixed in advance. Prompts are not added or dropped once results are seen.
  • Every run is logged, favorable or not. A query where the business is absent is recorded with the same care as one where it wins. Cherry-picking is the single easiest way to make this kind of report worthless.
  • Location context is held constant across a test cycle and recorded, because it materially changes results.
  • Non-fits are labeled as non-fits. One prompt below asks for a physician-owned practice. Meridian is nurse-practitioner owned, so its absence is a correct result, not a failure. Counting that as a miss would inflate the problem.
  • Exact text is preserved. Paraphrasing a prompt between cycles breaks comparability.

The Competitor E finding

Competitor E has 27 Google reviews. Meridian has 214 — roughly eight times as many — and outranks it on no query tested. E's advantage is not authority, spend, or review volume. This is the single most encouraging finding in the report, because the fix costs nothing.

Competitors A and C are worth a second look for a different reason: both publish their own category roundups. In this market, several of the "best of" lists that rank for the head term were written by competitors, who unsurprisingly rank themselves first. That is a legitimate, if self-serving, content strategy, and it is available to Meridian too — see §12.

The hours contradiction is the one to fix first

Row 10 is a business contradicting itself inside a single page load. The visible text says one thing; the machine-readable markup on the same page says another; the Google Business Profile says a third. When sources disagree, a retrieval system has to pick — and the one it is least likely to pick is the one no source corroborates. A patient told the clinic is open Saturday afternoon who arrives to a closed door is a review risk on top of a visibility one.

Row 11 carries regulatory weight

A third-party page states the practitioner is a physician. She is an APRN with a clear, active license. Neither Meridian nor AnswerFoundry authored that text, and there is no suggestion Meridian caused it. But in a regulated profession, an uncorrected public claim of a credential the practitioner does not hold is the kind of thing that is far cheaper to fix than to explain later. See §14.

The one source type where Meridian has a clean, claimed presence — the device manufacturer's provider directory — is the mechanism behind the single query it wins outright. That is the pattern to replicate, not a coincidence.

Where the real gains are

The instinct here is "publish more." That is the slow path. Three faster moves come first:

  1. Say what you already are. Every attribute in §8 is true today and unpublished. Rewriting eight page titles and adding one honest positioning paragraph addresses query 10 without producing a single new article.
  2. Put the head terms in the head positions. The injectables page cannot answer an injectables query when the term is absent from its title, heading, and URL.
  3. Move the reviews on-page. 214 reviews already exist. Rendering a subset as real HTML with valid markup converts an existing asset from invisible to citable — no new content required.

Only after those does a blog earn its cost. And when it does, the highest-value first post is the one the competitors already proved works in this market: an honest, genuinely useful category roundup.

20 Limitations and no-guarantee disclaimer

What this report is

A structured record of what publicly available sources said about one business on one day, and a professional assessment of the signals most likely influencing that. It is a diagnosis and a plan, not a prediction.

What was not tested

  • No conversational AI assistant was queried for this sample. Findings come from web search results, Google Maps, and direct inspection of sources — the retrieval substrate assistants draw on, which is a reasonable proxy but is not a measurement of assistant behavior.
  • Apple Maps, Bing Places, and the national provider registry were not checked.
  • Traffic, ranking history, backlink profile, and Search Console data were unavailable — no client access.
  • The identity of one out-of-area medical-directory profile is a name-and-city match only, and is not confirmed.
  • Whether the site's sitemap image paths resolve was not verified.
  • Current in-practice pricing was not confirmed with the business.

Inherent limitations of AI visibility testing

  • AI outputs change. The same prompt run twice, minutes apart, can return different answers. Any single result is a snapshot, not a measurement.
  • Results vary by date, model version, location, personalization, session history, and platform.
  • These systems are opaque. Correlation between a change and a shift in mentions can be observed; causation generally cannot be proven, and this report does not claim it.
  • Third-party data corrections are requests, not commands. Platforms accept, reject, or ignore them on their own timelines.
  • A single business in a single metro does not establish that any of this generalizes.
